  <div style="background:#06297a;overflow:hidden;margin-bottom:24px;"><img src="https://i0.wp.com/staging-131e-indiedocjourney.wpcomstaging.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/FB-Land-Before-Time-1024x572.jpeg?quality=72&amp;w=1000" alt="Dinosaurs gathered at a glowing portal, the land before the Facebook ban" loading="lazy" style="width:100%;display:block;"></div>
  <div style="column-count:2;column-gap:38px;font-size:16px;line-height:1.6;">
    <p style="margin:0 0 16px;"><span style="float:left;font-family:'Lato',sans-serif;font-weight:900;font-size:64px;line-height:.72;padding:6px 10px 0 0;color:#eb19cc;">B</span>ack in 2007, Facebook was the Digital Xanadu. It was a warm little island where normal people organized cool parties, and then the parties actually happened, like a lost season of <em>Happy Days</em> that somehow had Wi-Fi. People liked each other. People tagged each other. This is not nostalgia. This is an ontological fact, like gravity, or how every bar in Los Feliz has a candle that smells like "therapy."</p>
    <p style="margin:0 0 16px;">Facebook Events used to mean one thing. Party. Now "FB Event" conjures images of crisis management, Amway and militia recruitment. Right now most of them are like this:</p>
    <p style="margin:0 0 16px;font-family:'Roboto',sans-serif;font-size:18px;line-height:1.4;color:#9c1f8e;border-left:4px solid #eb19cc;padding-left:16px;">"Come to my cousin's live stream where he explains the ties between nootropics and homosexual amphibians."</p>
    <p style="margin:0 0 16px;">Now when you scroll Facebook you'll see 900 comments that read like <em>All in the Family</em> got trapped inside an iPad with Russian-bot Al Bundy and the next Ted Bundy.</p>
    <p style="margin:0;">But back in 2005, you clicked "Attending" and you attended. It was like <em>Superbad</em> but without the third act where everyone cries and vomits behind a Honda Civic. You showed up, saw people you recognized from profile photos taken on a Motorola Razr, drank something out of a red cup, and felt briefly like your life made sense.</p>

  <h2 style="font-family:'Lato',sans-serif;font-weight:900;font-size:34px;line-height:1.05;color:#06297a;margin:0 0 20px;">Facebook, a.k.a. the Boomer Thunderdome</h2>
  <div style="column-count:2;column-gap:38px;font-size:16px;line-height:1.6;">
    <p style="margin:0 0 16px;">That is how it goes. First the names get slippery. Then the vibes get slippery. Then your grandparents join. Presently, Facebook is the place you go to watch Boomers scream at everybody and no one. In reality, they are screaming at themselves. It's like <em>Who's Afraid of Virginia Woolf?</em> except the set is a comment section under a local news story about a sinkhole.</p>
    <p style="margin:0 0 16px;">I have a theory: Boomers missed their own online cringe era. Nobody archived their AIM away-message poetry. Nobody ratioed their "I'm just being honest" phase. So now they are doing it at 67, in public, with the confidence of a person who has never been screenshot by a goth furry and does not know that is a thing that happens.</p>
    <p style="margin:0 0 6px;font-family:'Archivo',sans-serif;font-size:12px;font-weight:800;letter-spacing:.22em;text-transform:uppercase;color:#b3850a;">Romanticizing the ex</p>
    <p style="margin:0;">When the Facebook ban hit, I did what any sane person does when a long relationship ends. I romanticized the beginning. In my head, Facebook turned back into the 2007 version, like an ex from college. I wanted the parties. And the feeling that my life was archived somewhere safe. I did not want what Facebook is now, essentially a wasteland of angst and whatever gross AI-generated thing the algorithm coughs up to monetize the human dopamine delivery system so Meta can hit their Q3 numbers.</p>

  <h2 style="font-family:'Lato',sans-serif;font-weight:900;font-size:34px;line-height:1.05;color:#06297a;margin:0 0 22px;">The smoking gun was the login log</h2>
  <div style="column-count:2;column-gap:38px;font-size:16px;line-height:1.6;">
    <p style="margin:0 0 16px;">More importantly, they include a detailed log of every time you've ever logged in. They also show the IP addresses linked to those logins.</p>
    <p style="margin:0 0 16px;">That file was the smoking gun. Buried in the login data was clear evidence of unauthorized access. IP addresses from locations I'd never been. Login times when I was provably elsewhere. Geographic impossibilities that would have required me to teleport between continents.</p>
    <p style="margin:0 0 16px;">So the data told a clear story. Someone had gained unauthorized access through <a href="https://owasp.org/www-community/attacks/Session_hijacking_attack" style="color:#9c1f8e;text-decoration:underline;">session-token theft</a>, the same stolen-credentials horror show I unpacked in <a href="https://indiedocjourney.com/2025/02/17/ghosting-and-passwords-a-modern-tale/" style="color:#9c1f8e;text-decoration:underline;">my modern tale of ghosting and passwords</a>.</p>
    <p style="margin:0 0 16px;">Here's how cookie theft actually works, minus the jargon. When you log in, the site hands your browser a little cryptographic file, a session cookie, that works like the VIP wristband you get after the bouncer checks your ID. Every time you click around, the site just glances at the wristband instead of asking for your password again.</p>
    <p style="margin:0 0 16px;">A hacker doesn't storm Meta's billion-dollar servers; they steal <em>your</em> wristband, usually through something dumb like a sketchy "dark mode" browser extension that quietly copies your cookies and ships them overseas. Then they paste it into their own browser, Facebook sees a valid wristband, and waves them past your password <em>and</em> your two-factor. That's why 2FA didn't save me: it guards the front door, and the thief climbed in through a window I didn't know was open.</p>
    <p style="margin:0;">As a result, my account was automatically disabled by their security systems. In other words, Facebook banned me for being the victim of a crime. Still, the real tragedy was in the content itself.</p>

The only human Meta sent, the now-legendary girl-baby-voiced counsel in the red turtleneck, showed up <strong>late.</strong> Then apologized and, in the same breath, noted that <em>I</em> had once shown up to the wrong courthouse. If they failed, judgment for me, <strong>$179.88 plus costs.</strong> February 18, 2026, 8:30 AM, Department 304.</p>

  <h4 style="font-family:'Lato',sans-serif;font-weight:900;font-size:30px;color:#c2f104;margin:0 0 22px;">Credit where credit is due, a Faboosh footnote</h4>
  <p style="font-size:17px;line-height:1.65;margin:0 0 22px;color:#dfe2ee;">I did not invent the idea of a digital bill of rights. I just put it in a gold toga and handed it a disco ball. The due-process spine of my list (a reason, evidence, a human, a real appeal) comes almost straight from the <a href="https://santaclaraprinciples.org/" style="color:#f5c518;">Santa Clara Principles</a> on content moderation, the framework the EFF and a coalition of civil-society groups built for exactly this kind of account-execution nightmare. And the "bill of rights" framing stands on bigger shoulders: Tim Berners-Lee, who invented the web and then asked for a "Magna Carta" to protect it; Senator Ron Wyden, who called for a Digital Bill of Rights back in 2012; and Representative Ro Khanna, whose Internet Bill of Rights earned Berners-Lee's blessing. They wrote the white papers. I wrote the show tunes. Cheers, sweeties.</p>
